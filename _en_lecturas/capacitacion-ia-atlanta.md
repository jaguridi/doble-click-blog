## At a glance

- **What it is:** *(404) Job Not Found: What Workforce Training Can't Fix for Black Atlantans in the Age of AI*
- **Who:** [Anuli Akanegbu](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Anuli+Akanegbu%22), a cultural anthropologist at Data & Society who lives in Atlanta.
- **Where:** Data & Society Research Institute, 2026, available on its website. [doi.org/10.69985/OOTF6452](https://doi.org/10.69985/OOTF6452).
- **Type:** ethnographic report. It combines participant observation, semi-structured interviews, and document analysis. Before publication it was read by community reviewers and peers, not by an academic journal.

## First reading: what it does and what it finds

A Georgia State University graduate looking for her first job told the author that earning a certificate for using ChatGPT seemed absurd to her. The scene captures the report's question: what it means to be seen as AI literate, and how that perception weighs on the career paths of Black workers in Atlanta.

The field material is this:

- six months of fieldwork, from February to August 2025;
- ten sites, including public institutions, companies, nonprofit organizations, conferences, and community training programs;
- 34 semi-structured interviews, lasting 15 to 90 minutes, with program participants, instructors, employers, officials, and administrators.

Akanegbu starts with a conceptual problem. She argues that AI literacy (*AI literacy*) has no clear definition, and she calls that vagueness *strategic abstraction*: the skill is demanded without saying what it consists of, and the task of interpreting it falls to the worker. In her fieldwork, several workers did not know whether it meant programming, using a chatbot, handling data, or including the right keywords in their résumé.

Her study also found that courses devoted solely to AI appeared mostly in office settings, offered by employers to their own staff. In programs for working-class adults, AI came in as a secondary topic within courses on digital literacy, cybersecurity, or job readiness. There it was presented as an aid for job hunting, for example to get past the automated filters that screen résumés.

The finding behind the title lies in those programs. The author describes free courses held Monday through Friday during working hours, which exclude anyone who works full time. One organization sometimes provides a monthly stipend of $200, which its own career counselor considers insufficient to stop working full time. Many participants arrived with years of experience in other sectors, but the programs steered them toward entry-level jobs. None of the programs she observed offered guaranteed employment, and according to the author, employers still preferred four-year college degrees. The report also devotes a section to DataWorks, at Georgia Tech, the only program the author found that pays its participants to work and learn on the job, with health insurance included.

What the fieldwork allows her to claim is a mechanism. In Atlanta in 2025, the obstacles the author saw recur were the cost of taking the courses, not knowing they existed, and the distance between finishing one and getting a job. The report does not measure how many participants find employment, nor does it compare outcomes across programs.

## Second reading: from Latin America

Two of the report's mechanisms can be tested with data from the region. These are the blog's questions, not the report's conclusions.

The first is the cost of training. Akanegbu portrays free courses whose schedule overlaps with the workday. To find out whether something similar happens in the region, one would need to compare dropout rates in public courses between people with full-time jobs and those without, and see whether it changes when the course includes a payment that compensates for the hours. In Chile, [SENCE](https://sence.gob.cl) administers publicly funded training; in Colombia, [SENA](https://www.sena.edu.co) delivers it. Their participant records would be the place to look for that data.

The second is where graduates end up. The report observed that the programs steered experienced adults toward entry-level jobs. The useful measurement would be formal employment and wages one year after completion, among those who arrived with prior experience, compared with their own income before the course. The author herself recommends that programs track those outcomes and break them down by race, class, gender, and neighborhood, instead of counting only enrollees and graduates.

The report's racial axis has a history specific to the southern United States. Each country in the region would have to decide which inequality shapes access to training: territory, gender, informality, or origin. Without that disaggregated data, an AI program could report how many people enrolled without saying whose work it changed.

## The fine print

- Recruitment was in person, by referral and, to a lesser extent, by email. The author explains that she focused on the workers she encountered most in the field. In a qualitative study that is not a flaw: its goal is to explain, not to estimate frequencies.
- Visits ranged from one to three per site, and the report does not claim to cover every initiative in Atlanta.
- The 97% certification rate of a cybersecurity program is reported by the program's own leadership; the report does not verify it.
- Several contextual figures, such as unionization in Georgia or graduate unemployment, come from other sources the report cites, not from its fieldwork.
- Funding: Robert Wood Johnson Foundation.
- One city, six months of fieldwork, and a method that explains mechanisms: the report indicates what to measure, not how much each barrier weighs.
