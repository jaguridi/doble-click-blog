## At a glance

- **Study:** *Generative AI in Developing Countries: Adoption Dynamics in Vietnamese Local Government*.
- **Team:** [Nguyen Duy Phu](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Nguyen+Duy+Phu%22), of the College of Local Administration at Khon Kaen University, in Thailand, with [Charles Ruangthamsing](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Charles+Ruangthamsing%22), [Peerasit Kamnuansilpa](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Peerasit+Kamnuansilpa%22), and [Grichawat Lowatcharin](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Grichawat+Lowatcharin%22), of the same college, and [Prasongchai Setthasuravich](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Prasongchai+Setthasuravich%22), of the University of Tokyo, the corresponding author.
- **Journal:** [*Informatics*](https://doi.org/10.3390/informatics13020022), from MDPI, volume 13, article 22, published on January 28, 2026, in open access. The text does not mention an earlier version.
- **Design:** a qualitative, exploratory study, with semi-structured interviews and reflexive thematic analysis.

## First reading: what it does and what it finds

Binh Duong was not chosen for being typical. The authors describe it as one of the provinces leading Vietnam's digital transformation, and they treat it as an extreme case: if the obstacles showed up there, they reason, they would probably weigh as much or more on local governments with fewer resources. To see which organizational factors encourage or hold back the use of generative AI in administrative work, they interviewed provincial officials and organized what they heard with the Technology, Organization, and Environment (TOE) framework, which sorts adoption factors into those three layers.

The fieldwork numbers are few:

- 25 interviews, conducted in June 2025;
- each one lasted 30 to 75 minutes;
- many interviewees estimate that the tool saves them between 30 and 50% of the time spent drafting official documents or summarizing information, a figure that is their perception and not a measurement.

According to the study, use neither became institutionalized nor stayed at zero. It occupies an unofficial, voluntary, and individual zone, which the team interprets as a condition produced by the structure and not as a passing stage. It is pushed along by ease of use and encouragement from supervisors. It is held back by four gaps the interviewees repeat: there are no implementation plans, there is no budget, the tools do not connect with government systems, and the provincial training, Bình dân học AI, is described as basic. Some pay for the subscription out of their own pocket, which, the paper warns, reserves the more capable versions for those who can afford them.

The piece with its own name is what they call the *AI accountability vacuum*. It brings together three fears: leaking state information on commercial platforms, working without rules on which data and tasks are allowed, and not knowing who is answerable if the tool gets it wrong. This last one, they conclude, is the central barrier. "If mistakes occur, employees will take all responsibility," one interviewee sums up. From there comes shadow use, with personal accounts and often without telling supervisors, and the habit of checking every generated sentence.

With that picture, the paper reinterprets the reluctance of officials over 45, which several interviewees mention: it presents it as rational resistance to a personal risk with no backing, not as technophobia. It also records a less expected fear, that of being replaced not by AI but by colleagues who handle it better.

What these interviews support is a mechanism, not a frequency: in a province with good conditions, enthusiasm coexisted with a lack of rules, resources, and people in charge, and the officials recount that this mix led them to use the tool outside official channels.

## Second reading: from Latin America

In Colombia, two bodies have already written the rule whose absence paralyzed the officials in Binh Duong. [Circular 023 of 2025](https://normativa.colpensiones.gov.co/compilacion/docs/circular_contraloria_0023_2025.htm) of the Office of the Comptroller General of the Republic, which regulates the use of AI within that agency, establishes that each public servant and contractor will always be responsible for the content, accuracy, and relevance of documents. The Bogotá Mayor's Office, according to [Forbes Colombia](https://forbes.co/2026/03/04/ia/alcaldia-de-bogota-prohibe-a-sus-funcionarios-poner-datos-sensibles-en-herramientas-abiertas-de-ia/), prohibited entering personal data or classified information into open tools and placed on the public servant the responsibility for what they produce with the help of AI.

Both rules fill the regulatory vacuum the paper describes: they say which data stay out and who is answerable. But they resolve the ambiguity by assigning the risk to the individual, which is the situation the paper identifies as the most paralyzing and which the authors propose turning into a collective function of the organization. In this blog's view, clarity has value, because it removes doubt about what counts as a violation. Whether the rational resistance the study describes gives way or persists would depend on what is missing in Binh Duong: approved tools paid for by the institution, instead of personal accounts.

In Chile, the Ministry of Labor and Sofofa [expanded to the public sector](https://www.mintrab.gob.cl/ministerio-del-trabajo-y-sofofa-amplian-programa-hazlo-con-ia-al-sector-publico-con-32-mil-cupos-gratuitos-de-formacion/) the Hazlo con IA program, with 32,000 free training slots. It is a bet similar to Bình dân học AI, whose courses the interviewees describe as basic: they ask for training to judge which data not to enter and to check the quality of what is generated. Measuring whether the Chilean version goes further would require comparing those who took it with those who did not, according to how many use institutional tools and under which internal rules.

The [IDB reported](https://www.iadb.org/en/blog/modernization-state/public-administration/individual-ai-institutional-transformation-public-sector) an exploratory survey of officials from 19 countries in the region, which the bank itself states is not representative. How many of them work with a tool their institution pays for and backs, and how many with their own?

## The fine print

- Snowball recruitment, with at least two years of service. The team considers saturation reached around interview 20.
- One researcher coded and a second reviewed all the material. In a qualitative design, sample size is not a flaw: it seeks to explain mechanisms, not to measure frequencies.
- The 45-year threshold comes from what the interviewees say; the participant table reports position and years of service, not age.
- No one timed tasks: the time savings and their erosion by checking are perceptions and inferences.
- On July 1, 2025, after the fieldwork, Binh Duong merged with Ba Ria–Vung Tau and Ho Chi Minh City.
- No external funding or declared conflicts. The staged recommendations are proposals; they were not tested.
- The finding describes one province and one moment; generalizing it would require comparing different local governments.
