## At a glance

- **What it is:** *Are We Ready for AI? From Measurement to Policy Governance*, technical note IDB-TN-03318.
- **Who:** [Eduardo Levy Yeyati](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Eduardo+Levy+Yeyati%22), of the School of Government at Universidad Torcuato di Tella and of the IDB. He is the sole author.
- **Where:** Inter-American Development Bank, Productivity, Trade and Innovation sector, March 2026, under an open CC BY 4.0 license.
- **Type:** institutional technical note. It combines a descriptive analysis of seven international indexes with a measurement proposal. It does not state any external peer review.

## First reading: what it does and what it finds

Depending on which ranking you consult, the world leader in AI readiness may be Singapore, Estonia, or China. That is how Levy Yeyati opens his note, which then measures how much the indexes that governments and donors use to compare themselves disagree.

He reviews seven: those of Oxford Insights, Tortoise Media, Stanford HAI, the OECD, the IMF, the AGILE index, and the Global Index on Responsible AI (GIRAI). These indexes mix, in different doses, two ideas that the note separates: the current capacity to adopt AI (*readiness*) and the capacity to adapt to its effects (*preparedness*).

The main tool is the correlation between the scores of each pair of indexes. On that scale, 1 would mean that two indexes score countries in perfect proportion and 0 that their scores bear no relation; as our own approximation, a 0.33 indicates weak agreement. The results:

- the average correlation among the seven is 33%, and it rises to 47% when one index is excluded;
- Oxford Insights and the IMF agree at 0.93, and Tortoise and Stanford, at 0.88;
- the OECD and Tortoise are almost unrelated (0.05);
- GIRAI moves in the opposite direction from Stanford (−0.44);
- even the innovation and research measures, compared across six indexes, average 58%.

By country, the dispersion is also large: Estonia ranks fifth for the IMF and 32nd for Tortoise, and India's position varies by 61 places across indexes.

For the region, the note estimates a regression with per capita income as the only control. In four of the seven indexes, being in Latin America and the Caribbean is associated with a lower score than that of countries with similar income; in the Oxford Insights index, the difference is about 9 points, and in GIRAI, almost 13. The author interprets this as suggesting a systematic undervaluation of the region.

From there he derives five structural problems: different definitions of what it means to be prepared, data that do not see informal innovation or innovation documented mainly in Spanish or Portuguese, time lags, uniform indicators for different contexts, and rewards for the visible over institutional transformation. The last one supports his governance argument: rankings carry weight for reputation and financing, so governments could "invest for the index."

The proposal is a dashboard, the Adaptive AI Readiness Scorecard (AARS). It would measure gaps against benchmarks from similar countries, with modules that each country chooses according to its strategy, with no ranking and no link to financing.

The note establishes that seven influential rankings do not measure the same thing and that their discrepancies are not mere statistical noise. That the region is undervalued remains an association the author interprets, not a demonstrated bias.

## Second reading: from Latin America

The note's regression table contains a finding that strains its own regional narrative. The text attributes the undervaluation to rankings dominated by research and commercialization, which would not reward the ethical frameworks of Brazil and Chile. But the largest regional penalty appears in GIRAI, the index based on human rights. In Tortoise and Stanford, those with a commercial and academic profile, the regional coefficient is not significant, although Tortoise includes only seven countries from the region, and that limits what can be detected.

For this blog, that result changes the question for Brazil and Chile. If the region lags furthest behind precisely in the index that measures rights, the ethical approach the note credits them with does not explain the gap on its own, and the explanation would have to be sought within GIRAI. The appendix uses two of its components, government frameworks and government actions. Comparing the two countries' scores on each would show whether the lag lies in the written rules or in their enforcement, and each answer would point to different reforms.

On Uruguay, the note states that its digital government scores modestly because the indexes overweight publications and patents, but it does not test this. Its own data would make it possible: Uruguay is in the Tortoise sample, and Oxford Insights, focused on public sector capacity, is the index with the widest coverage. If the thesis is correct, its position in Oxford should be considerably better than in Tortoise.

The proposal leaves the IDB as coordinator of a pilot of the dashboard, without assessing or scoring countries, and adds a composite index, CoRPI, built mostly from the subcomponents of these same rankings. In the governance pillar, the appendix shows correlations among those subcomponents ranging from negative to very high. When two measure the same thing, CoRPI keeps one and uses the other as a check, without saying by what criterion it chooses. That rule would influence the position of each country in the region, and a pilot would have to set it before comparing.

## The fine print

- The correlations use official scores from the 2023 to 2025 editions, only for the countries common to each pair; the appendix warns that coefficients with different samples are not directly comparable.
- There are internal inconsistencies. The body says the 47% excludes GIRAI, and the appendix says it excludes AGILE. The text presents AGILE as more favorable to the region, but its figure 1 shows 58 versus 77 there, a larger gap than in Oxford Insights (50 versus 56).
- The regression does not separate measurement bias from a real capacity gap.
- The cases of Uruguay, Chile, and Brazil are illustrative.
- Conflict of interest: it is an IDB note that relies on the bank's own digital transformation framework and assigns it the coordination of the pilot.
- It serves as evidence that the rankings disagree; it is not enough to state how much they undervalue the region.
