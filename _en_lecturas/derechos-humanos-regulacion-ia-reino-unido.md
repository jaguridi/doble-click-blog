## At a glance

- **What it is:** *Human Rights and the Regulation of AI*, fourth report of the 2026-27 session (HC 160 / HL Paper 56).
- **Who:** the [Joint Committee on Human Rights](https://www.parliament.uk/jchr), a joint committee of the House of Commons and the House of Lords, chaired by Labour MP Alex Sobel. The specialist adviser was [Karen Yeung](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Karen+Yeung%22), of the University of Birmingham.
- **Where:** UK Parliament, published on September 14, 2026, freely reproducible under the Open Parliament Licence.
- **Type:** report of a parliamentary inquiry, based on written evidence and hearings. It is not an empirical study: its conclusions are the committee's judgments about what it heard.

## First reading: what it does and what it finds

The committee opened its inquiry in July 2025 with one question: whether British law and regulation are enough to protect human rights in the face of AI. It received more than 70 pieces of written evidence and held ten oral evidence sessions, with academics, civil society organizations, regulators, Google, Meta, Microsoft, and the AI minister. From the Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI it took the rights it focused on: equality and non-discrimination, privacy and data protection, and the right to an effective remedy.

The central diagnosis concerns who is accountable. According to the report, the British laws that could apply to AI operate mainly at the point of use, so responsibility falls on whoever deploys a system and not on whoever designed it. The committee considers that this probably allows harms that could have been prevented. It adds that developers can contractually pass risks on to smaller companies, which have no access to the system's components.

Two concrete gaps follow. Before launch, except in very limited cases such as medical devices, regulators cannot test a system or stop it from reaching the market. The AI Security Institute (AISI), the public body that evaluates advanced models, accesses them only through voluntary agreements and has no legal powers. After launch, the law does not require telling a person that an AI system was involved in a decision about them. Without that notice, the committee reasons, no one can challenge the decision, and the evidence it received strongly suggests that many people would be in that situation. The government's Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard (ATRS) is mandatory for ministries, not for local councils or police forces, and the report describes it as limited in its coverage.

The recommendations respond to that diagnosis:

- an AI law, promised in the 2024 King's Speech and not yet introduced, with obligations graduated by risk;
- prohibitions, such as emotion inference or subliminal techniques, which would also extend to the development of very powerful systems, such as general AI and superintelligence, when they risk serious and widespread harm;
- prior approval for systems that pose a high risk to human rights;
- an independent oversight body, with a public register of incidents;
- an AISI on a statutory footing, to which developers would have to submit their new models for evaluation.

The committee also calls for data regulation to make clear that the mere presence of a person in the loop does not amount to meaningful human involvement.

The report puts forward a legal and political judgment about the gaps in British law, supported by consistent testimony. It does not measure how many harms occur or how often.

## Second reading: from Latin America

The report brings its own regional data point. In footnote 35, Uruguay appears among the signatories of the Framework Convention, alongside the United Kingdom, the United States, and the European Union. The convention enters into force with five ratifications, and signing is not the same as ratifying.

The British case shows where the next step gets stuck. The government told the committee that its legal framework was already consistent with the convention's principles. The committee, however, concluded that current law is not enough to ensure those affected the effective remedy the convention requires in its Article 14. In the blog's view, Uruguay would face a similar question before ratifying: whether its legislation requires informing a person when an AI system takes part in a decision that affects them. If that obligation does not exist, that would be the first gap to review, because according to the committee there is no possible challenge without notice.

Public sector transparency offers a comparison with Chile. An [OECD and GPAI report](https://wp.oecd.ai/app/uploads/2024/12/15-Algorithmic-Transparency-in-the-Public-Sector-Case-studies-of-repositories-of-public-algorithms-in-Chile-the-EU-and-the-UK.pdf) studies as cases the public algorithms repository of Universidad Adolfo Ibáñez and the British register. The British register is mandatory for ministries, and even so the committee finds it limited: it leaves out local councils and police forces, and one witness stated that not every algorithmic use appears in it. An academic repository depends on what institutions report or on what its researchers find. What would need to be measured in Chile is what share of the algorithms used by municipalities and police appears in that repository, checked against a direct survey of a sample of those institutions.

For a signatory country, the British diagnosis works better as a checklist than as a model. Its first box is who is required to notify the affected person.

## The fine print

- The conclusions rest on testimony. The figure of some 3 million people whose faces the police reportedly scanned between January and October 2025 is an estimate the committee says it heard, not its own measurement.
- There was no unanimity: the EHRC, the equality regulator, warned that a single regulator would duplicate and complicate existing regulation, and regulators did not raise gaps on their own initiative. Meta praised the government's approach.
- The evidence was taken under the previous government. A new prime minister took office at the close, and the report acknowledges that the approach may change.
- Intellectual property and justice were left out of focus.
- Transparency: the report mentions Anthropic, the company that develops Claude, the model that writes this section.
- Scope: a map of gaps in British law, useful as a guide to questions, not as a measurement of harms.
