## At a glance

- **What it is:** *The Rise of AI Authorities? A Closer Look at Latin America's Institutional Responses and External Influences*.
- **Who:** [Rocco Saverino](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Rocco+Saverino%22), [Pablo Trigo Kramcsák](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Pablo+Trigo+Kramcs%C3%A1k%22) and [Barbara da Rosa Lazarotto](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Barbara+da+Rosa+Lazarotto%22), doctoral researchers in the LSTS group at the Vrije Universiteit Brussel (VUB). According to their authorship statement, Trigo Kramcsák, who holds a law degree from the Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile, led the Chilean section, and da Rosa Lazarotto, the Brazilian one.
- **Where:** [University of Bologna Law Review](https://doi.org/10.60923/issn.2531-6133/21439), published on March 19, 2026, with stated dates of receipt, revision and acceptance. The PDF does not indicate an access license.
- **Type:** comparative legal analysis of laws and bills, with no new data. An editorial note warns that several initiatives changed while the article was being edited.

## First reading: what it does and what it finds

The European AI regulation requires each member state to designate national authorities, and the authors identify three ways to do so: create new agencies, designate existing bodies or form hybrid competence centers. Europe already shows the divergence. Spain created AESIA, on whose governing board all members but one are tied to a ministry. The Italian bill splits the task between two government agencies and leaves out the personal data Garante. In France, the Council of State proposed turning the CNIL into the AI regulator.

With that grid, the article examines Latin America. It argues that AI regulation will probably follow the path of data protection, which arrived in waves inspired by Europe and adapted to each country. The contextual data it gathers are these:

- the first wave of data laws was that of Chile (1999), Argentina and Paraguay (2000);
- the second came between 2008 and 2012, from Uruguay to Colombia;
- after the European data regulation came Brazil (2018), Ecuador (2021) and the Chilean reform of 2024;
- only Argentina and Uruguay have an adequacy decision from the European Union;
- of the AI initiatives the article tabulates in Peru, Argentina, Uruguay, Colombia and Ecuador, the only one approved is Peru's Law 31814, from July 2023.

Brazil and Chile are the case studies. In Brazil, bill 2338/2023 creates a National AI Regulation and Governance System (SIA), and recent amendments left the data authority, the ANPD, as its coordinator. The authors warn of a constitutional risk. Amendment 115/2022 settled the Union's competence over personal data, and they anticipate that the same debate may arise with AI and require another reform.

In Chile, a 2023 parliamentary bill required prior authorization from a national commission to develop or use any AI system. In May 2024 it was merged with the Executive's bill, which proposes a technical advisory council and hands enforcement to the new Personal Data Protection Agency. According to the article, that agency was due to begin operating in October 2026, lacks expertise in product safety and its capacity to oversee AI is uncertain.

The conclusion is cautious. The authors consider it unlikely that the European architecture will be reproduced in full, and they see Brazil and Chile apparently converging toward hybrid schemes. What is solid here is the legal map; the convergence is a reading of two bills that, when the manuscript was closed, were not law.

## Second reading: from Latin America

The Chilean timetable has already reached its date. The article places in October 2026, last month, the start of the Personal Data Protection Agency created by [Law 21.719](https://www.bcn.cl/leychile/navegar?idNorma=1209272), with full setup planned for December. If the [AI bill](https://www.camara.cl/legislacion/proyectosdeley/tramitacion.aspx?prmID=17429&prmBOLETIN=16821-19) advances as it stands, that same agency would add the enforcement of AI systems to a mandate the authors describe as centered on personal data.

Here a Doble Lectura hypothesis comes in. The conclusion recommends that large economies with developed institutional capacity, Chile among them, expand the mandate of their data authority. That prescription assumes precisely the capacity that the Chilean section of the same article calls into question. The way to settle it is empirical: look at the agency's staffing and budget in its first year and how much of that could go to evaluating AI systems.

In Brazil, the ANPD already has a point of entry that does not depend on the AI bill. The text notes that Article 20 of the LGPD allows it to audit discriminatory aspects of automated decisions, and that the authority published [Technical Note 12/2025](https://www.gov.br/anpd/pt-br/acesso-a-informacao/participacao-social/outras-acoes/documentos/ts-06-2024-nt-12-2025-consolidacao-das-contribuicoes.pdf/view), which summarizes the input from a public consultation to set interpretive parameters for that provision. Counting how many audits of that kind the ANPD has carried out would show whether the experience that justifies giving it coordination of the SIA exists in practice or only on paper.

There is a second Brazilian front. If the constitutional objection the authors anticipate succeeds, coordination of the system would be in dispute right at its launch, and the sectoral regulators the bill brings in would have to coordinate without a clear head, with the risk of diluting responsibilities that the article already points out.

In both countries, the institutional question is no longer which European model to copy. It is whether the data authority that would take on AI has the means to do it.

## The fine print

- The regional table excludes the Caribbean and does not state how the countries were chosen.
- The article was received in April 2025 and published in March 2026. The editorial note acknowledges legislative changes after the manuscript.
- There are internal inconsistencies. The conclusion describes Brazil as a sectoral approach that prefers non-binding instruments, while section 5.1 analyzes a bill with rights, risk classification and a coordinating authority. Table 1 assigns a Peruvian bill the number 2338/2023, the same as the Brazilian bill.
- The recommendations by economy size are not supported by capacity data, nor do they define what a large or medium economy is.
- No conflicts of interest declared; partial funding from the VUB.
- The article organizes the region's legal map up to 2025. Which authority design works best can only be judged once these agencies are enforcing.
