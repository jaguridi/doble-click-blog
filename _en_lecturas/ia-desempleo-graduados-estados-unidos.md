## At a glance

- **What it is:** *The Early Impacts of AI on Employment Among Recent College Graduates*
- **Who:** [Robert Fairlie](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Robert+Fairlie%22) (UCLA and NBER) and [Jane Wu](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Jane+Wu%22+UCLA) (UCLA).
- **Where:** CESifo Working Paper No. 12994, dated September 15, 2026, free to download from the CESifo website, SSRN and RePEc. It is a working paper, not peer reviewed, with data through August 2026.
- **Type:** descriptive and regression analysis using microdata from the Current Population Survey (CPS), the monthly household survey that produces the official US unemployment figures.

## First reading: what it does and what it finds

Every Northern Hemisphere summer, unemployment among US college students who have just graduated rises. According to the paper, between June and August it sits almost 2 percentage points above the 5% average for the rest of the year. That is why Fairlie and Wu do not compare summer 2026 with the spring, but with the same months of the previous four years. They look at people aged 22 to 25 whose highest degree is a bachelor's and who are no longer studying, between January 2022 and August 2026. In addition to official unemployment, they build a broader measure that adds those who did not look for work in the last four weeks but say they want a job. They call it *sidelined unemployment*: unemployment on the bench, so to speak.

The key numbers:

- Average unemployment from June to August was 7.3% in 2026, compared with 7.1% in 2022, 6.3% in 2023, 7.8% in 2024 and 7.2% in 2025.
- With the broader measure, 2026 reached 10.4%, the highest of the five summers, just 0.3 points above the previous high.
- In the main regression, which accounts for the prior trend, the coefficient for summer 2026 is -0.008, with a standard error of 0.007.

That coefficient is read in probability points: -0.008 equals 0.8 percentage points less unemployment than the trend anticipated. It is a small estimate, with a margin of error of the same size, and it is not statistically different from zero; nor is it with the broader measure or when comparing only with 2022.

The second finding comes from comparing with groups that, according to the authors, should be less exposed to AI. Relative to graduates aged 30 to 49, the gap in official unemployment remained stable between 2025 and 2026. Relative to young people of the same age without a college degree, graduates' advantage on the broader measure narrowed to its lowest level of the five summers. In the regressions, none of those differences is significant.

The third looks at occupations. The authors cross unemployment with two measures of AI exposure, one theoretical and another based on actual use of Claude. In all eight specifications the coefficients are positive and none is significant. With remote work, a positive relationship appears that they describe as marginally significant and hard to separate from AI exposure, because both measures move together.

With these data, something limited can be claimed. In the summer the authors chose as a first test, unemployment among new graduates stayed within the range of previous years. Fairlie and Wu caution that their work is descriptive and does not rule out larger effects for the cohorts of 2027 onward.

## Second reading: from Latin America

More than an exportable result, the paper leaves a method. What follows is the blog's interpretation: three questions the region would have to answer before concluding something similar.

The first is about the calendar. The study shows that the seasonal rise of almost two points, without comparing the same months across years, would be mistaken for an effect. In countries where the university year ends around December, such as Chile or Argentina, the equivalent window would not be June to August. A replication would first have to establish when each cohort enters the labor market in each country.

The second is what the surveys ask. The broader measure, which adds almost two points to official unemployment, is the one that hit the highest of the five summers in 2026, although without statistical significance. The region's household surveys, such as the National Employment Survey of [Chile's INE](https://www.ine.gob.cl), the Great Integrated Household Survey of [DANE](https://www.dane.gov.co) in Colombia, or the ENOE of [INEGI](https://www.inegi.org.mx) in Mexico, classify people according to whether they are looking for work and whether they are available. The point to check is whether their microdata make it possible to isolate, month by month or by quarter, young graduates who are no longer studying and who would like to work without looking.

The third is where the signal would appear. For the United States, the paper also builds a measure of mismatch: employed graduates in jobs that require no educational credential. The blog's hypothesis is that in markets with high informality, such as Colombia's or Mexico's, a graduate who cannot find a first formal job may end up in an informal or mismatched one before ending up unemployed. If AI were to reduce entry-level jobs, the effect might show up first in those indicators. The useful comparison would be the same as the paper's: graduates versus young people without a degree, in the same months of each year.

What is also missing is a measure of exposure designed for local occupations. The two the study uses were built with US classifications, and transferring them requires crosswalk tables that can add error.

## The fine print

- The paper does not mention a preregistered analysis, that is, one fixed before looking at the data so as not to pick the most convenient specification afterward. The null result holds when dropping 2025 from the data, removing the trend, widening the age range to 22-27, and redefining the comparison groups.
- The authors acknowledge that no comparison group is a clean control.
- The occupation analysis loses 15.5% of the unemployed and 35.8% of the broader measure, who have no reported occupation: precisely the group of greatest concern, the paper warns.
- Transparency: one of the exposure measures is the Anthropic Economic Index, built from the use of Claude, the family of models that writes this section.
- One summer, one country: enough to rule out a large, visible jump in 2026, not slow or concentrated effects.
