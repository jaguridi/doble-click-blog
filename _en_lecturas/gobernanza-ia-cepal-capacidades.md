## At a glance

- **Title:** *El estado de la gobernanza de la inteligencia artificial en América Latina y el Caribe*, with a subtitle that promises conceptual foundations, international frameworks, and national profiles.
- **Authors:** [Demetris Herakleous](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Demetris+Herakleous%22), of ECLAC's Digital Transformation Unit, with contributions from [Sebastián Rovira](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Sebasti%C3%A1n+Rovira%22) and [Alejandro Patiño](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Alejandro+Pati%C3%B1o%22), of the same commission.
- **Publication:** ECLAC, Project Documents series (LC/TS.2026/74), 2026, with an open-access [digital version](https://bit.ly/CEPAL2026-74S). The credits state that it did not undergo editorial review. It was prepared under an ECLAC project with GIZ, funded by Germany's Federal Ministry for Economic Cooperation and Development (BMZ).
- **Type of study:** a descriptive report, based on document review and third-party indices, with no primary data. The regulatory typology has a cutoff of January 2026, although some profiles record events from May 2026.

## First reading: what it does and what it finds

The document starts from a premise: governing AI is not limited to regulating it, because it also requires technical capacities, data, infrastructure, incentives, and coordination across sectors. With that yardstick, it covers international frameworks, the regional landscape, and a profile for each country, from Argentina to the U.S. Virgin Islands, including the dependent territories of the Caribbean.

To organize that map, it uses two tools. The first is the Latin American Artificial Intelligence Index (ILIA) 2025, produced by Chile's National Center for Artificial Intelligence (CENIA) and ECLAC itself. The second is a typology that classifies instruments, not countries: general laws, binding sector-specific rules, ethical frameworks without the force of law, bills under consideration, and references to AI within digital government laws. A single country can fall into several categories, and the author warns that the typology does not measure maturity. The key data:

- the ILIA 2025 assesses 19 countries and ranks Chile, Brazil, and Uruguay as pioneers; it divides the rest between adopters and explorers;
- as of January 2026, Peru, with Law 31814 of 2023 and its 2025 regulations, and El Salvador, with Legislative Decree 234 of 2025, are the clearest cases of a general AI law;
- Brazil committed about $4.5 billion for 2024-2028 and Colombia around 116 million; Uruguay, Chile, Costa Rica, the Dominican Republic, Peru, and Cuba do not specify overall amounts.

On the bills, the text describes lengthy deliberations. Brazil's, already approved by the Senate, faces delays in the Chamber of Deputies. The author sees a risk of obsolescence there: laws that, by the time they pass, no longer correspond to generative or autonomous systems. The author calls the lack of amounts "a tangible risk for implementation."

The overall diagnosis fits in one sentence from the final section: the regional problem is not the absence of guiding ideas, but the unequal distribution of the capacities to apply them. Seven recommendations follow from this. Among them are starting with the state's own use of AI, with algorithmic impact assessments and registries of high-risk systems; drafting model clauses for public AI procurement that do not wait for legislative reforms; and a "minimum viable capacity" in each country, with a national coordination core and costly technical functions, such as auditing, that can be shared at the regional level.

Within its scope, the report offers a dated inventory of instruments and a reasoned thesis about the implementation gap. That thesis is argued with cases and indices; no analysis in the document measures the relationship between state capacity and outcomes.

## Second reading: from Latin America

El Salvador makes it possible to test the thesis with the report's own tables. It has a general law and a dedicated agency, the National Artificial Intelligence Agency (ANIA), charged with overseeing compliance with the law and keeping a National Registry of AI Development, Innovation, and Application. Even so, the ILIA places it among the explorers, and in its governance dimension gives it 38.49 points, compared with 83.24 for Chile, which has no general law. The index is on a base of 100, and by this reading's calculation the Salvadoran score is less than half of the Chilean one. The report does not specify the date of the ILIA data, which could predate the law. The reading this text proposes is that the law created functions that require staff and budget, and that the report gives no figures for either one for the ANIA. Knowing whether the agency is closing that gap would require its executed budget, its technical staffing, and how many systems the registry has enrolled.

Colombia lends some support to the path the report proposes for not depending on Congress. Its AI bill, PL 043 of 2025, was still under consideration, but Joint Directive 007 of 2025 from the Ombudsman's Office and the Office of the Inspector General already introduces for the public sector, according to the document, information obligations and criteria for "meaningful explanation" in administrative procedures. If those requirements were carried into procurement specifications, an oversight directive would become a condition that the vendor signs, which is what the model clauses seek. The effect would depend on something the report does not measure: how many of the Colombian state's AI contracts include those obligations today.

In both cases, the data point that decides whether the thesis applies lies outside the inventory: not how many instruments a country has, but what resources and what contracts back them.

## The fine print

- The report does not detail how it compiled or verified the profiles; its tables are labeled "Own elaboration".
- There are internal inconsistencies. The Chilean bill appears as Bulletin 15869-19 in one chapter and 16821-19 in another; the Colombian amount, as both 116 and 120 million dollars.
- The text states that "This demonstrates" that the indices reflect, with a lag, prior governance, without an analysis that proves it.
- ECLAC co-produces the ILIA, its central framework, and several recommendations refer to the project's own documentation. This is internal evidence, not an independent evaluation.
- Taken beyond a dated inventory, the result no longer holds: how much of each strategy is implemented is another investigation.
