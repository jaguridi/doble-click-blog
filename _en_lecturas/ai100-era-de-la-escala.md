## At a glance

- **What it is:** *AI Goes Mainstream: The Scaling Era and the Rise of Generative AI*, the third report of the One Hundred Year Study on Artificial Intelligence (AI100).
- **Who:** a panel of twelve chaired by [Michael Wooldridge](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Michael+Wooldridge%22), of the University of Oxford, with [Peter Asaro](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Peter+Asaro%22), [Lucie Flek](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Lucie+Flek%22), [Iryna Gurevych](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Iryna+Gurevych%22), [Sara Hooker](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Sara+Hooker%22), [Gina Neff](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Gina+Neff%22), [Peter Norvig](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Peter+Norvig%22), [Raymond Perrault](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Raymond+Perrault%22), [Iyad Rahwan](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Iyad+Rahwan%22), [Samantha Shorey](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Samantha+Shorey%22), [Toby Walsh](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Toby+Walsh%22), and [Luke Zettlemoyer](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Luke+Zettlemoyer%22).
- **Where:** [Stanford University](http://ai100.stanford.edu/2026-report), November 2026, under a Creative Commons BY-ND 4.0 license. The project is run from Stanford HAI.
- **Type:** expert assessment, with no original data. It was written between late 2025 and September 2026, so it portrays the models available up to then.

## First reading: what it does and what it finds

Every five years, a new AI100 panel answers the same twelve questions about AI and its effects. This third edition adds two in-depth analyses, one on geopolitics and one on scale, and closes by reviewing what the 2021 report got right. It provides no new measurements; it organizes published evidence and the judgment of its authors.

The central thesis is that the advances of these five years come from building systems with ever more compute and more data. That paradigm, the panel argues, is already under pressure: new high-quality data is scarce, and the compute cost of frontier models has become prohibitive. Hence its expectation that the next phase will depend less on larger models and more on giving them time to reason during use, memory, tools, and agents.

Scale has a financial side, and that is where the report gathers its most striking figures, almost all taken from third parties:

- some estimates attribute half of US GDP growth in 2025 to investment in AI infrastructure;
- more than 15% of US stock market gains that year are attributed to the rise in Nvidia's stock alone;
- current investment in data centers is roughly triple, adjusted for inflation, what was invested in fiber optics in the late nineties.

Even so, the authors state that the economic impact does not yet show up in aggregate macroeconomic data or in company returns: "AI is not yet paying its way."

The diagnosis on governance is one of fragmentation. International governance is increasingly framed as national security, the summits that began at Bletchley Park left declarations without legal force, and the European Union postponed until late 2027 much of its rules for high-risk systems. The panel adds that the public has had little voice over a technology that is reorganizing the lives of billions, and it cites research suggesting that presenting AI as a replacement for human work discourages political participation.

Perhaps the most useful part is the self-criticism. The 2021 panel anticipated a transformation, but not generative AI. The 2026 authors suggest as a possible cause that the earlier group, made up almost entirely of people from the field, underestimated what continued scaling of the transformer architecture would deliver. The lesson they apply to themselves: when experts agree about what is coming, they may be agreeing within the limits of the previous paradigm.

The report offers, then, a qualified balance that is frank about its uncertainties. Its forecasts are worth as expert judgment, not as measured results.

## Second reading: from Latin America

Across its 127 pages, the region appears more as a setting than as a protagonist. The report mentions Chinese urban surveillance systems exported to Latin America, among other regions, often with financing included, and asks whether the recipients gain capacity or cede control. It records synthetic media aimed at elections in Argentina, with the caveat that there appears to be no evidence of large-scale electoral effects. And it picks up press reports according to which Claude, through Anthropic's partnership with Palantir, contributed to the January 2026 operation that captured Nicolás Maduro. The list of middle powers seeking a seat at the table, from the United Arab Emirates to India, includes no Latin American country.

The chapter on governance does offer a tool for reading a concrete regional decision; applying it to Chile is this section's own work. It compares two models: regulating the technology or expanding the powers of sectoral regulators. Of the second, it warns of two costs: that each sector may have to regulate the same harm, and that many of those regulators lack real expertise in AI.

The [Chilean AI bill](https://www.camara.cl/legislacion/proyectosdeley/tramitacion.aspx?prmID=17429&prmBOLETIN=16821-19) hands enforcement to the Personal Data Protection Agency created by [Law 21.719](https://www.bcn.cl/leychile/navegar?idNorma=1209272). If it moves forward on those terms, it would in principle avoid the first cost the report describes, because enforcement would not be split among sectors. The second, by contrast, would be concentrated in that agency: oversight would depend on its technical capacity.

There is also a generalization by the panel worth testing here: that societies in developing regions show more technological optimism than the West, a claim it supports with a 2023 Ipsos survey. Checking it would require comparing, with the same question, the share of people more excited than worried in Brazil, Mexico, or Colombia against the United States, and repeating it now that use has become widespread.

## The fine print

- Many figures come from others' estimates, industry surveys, or corporate announcements, such as the ability of Claude Mythos Preview to find vulnerabilities, taken from Anthropic's announcement.
- There is an internal tension about the economy: the key messages say the impact is not yet visible in aggregate data, while the review of the 2021 report argues that it was already emerging in productivity in 2025.
- According to the authors, the text was written by people; AI was used to transcribe, summarize, review drafts, and sometimes suggest rewordings.
- The panel's affiliations are in the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, and Australia.
- Transparency: the report discusses Anthropic products, including Claude, and this section is written by Claude, a model from Anthropic.
- As a record of its time and a map of debates it has value of its own; the effects it mentions should be sought, measured, in the studies it cites.
