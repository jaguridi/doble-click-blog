Doble Lectura, from Doble Click. Today, what happens when an artificial intelligence that draws gets involved in designing a park.

A team of researchers partnered with a landscape architecture firm in Los Angeles to improve a real park. The idea was simple: instead of showing residents images made in advance, generate images live, with artificial intelligence, from what each person was describing. That is how they interviewed nine residents, children of Latin American migrants.

And they found something they did not expect. The technically perfect images, the ones that came out faithful to what was asked for, often shut the conversation down. The person would say "yes, that's it," and that was the end of it. By contrast, the imperfect images, sometimes inaccurate or frankly strange, were the ones that opened the conversation up. One participant could not name an element of her Mexican heritage until she saw a mural in an image and remembered her mother's stories. For another, a fanciful park made of mushrooms helped him imagine a more natural design. The inaccuracy, here, was the engine, not the flaw.

The second thing they saw is that having an image in front of them, even a wrong one, made people describe the space much better: where each thing went, where they placed themselves, what atmosphere they wanted. And the third, which sets a limit on all of the above: the outcome depended heavily on the skill of whoever was facilitating the conversation. The tool alone does not do the work.

Why does this matter from Latin America? The participants were diaspora, children of migrants in the United States, not people living in the region. And that is where the most interesting tension appears: the images tended to look idealized and very American, and it was hard to represent real cultural heritage. Stereotypes appeared. One participant associated his idea of Mexico with the movie Encanto, which is actually inspired by Colombia. But the authors turn this around: a biased image can serve to talk about the bias itself, to name what is missing and what does not look like oneself.

And there is a question that gives pause. The study used tools from 2024, which still made quite a few mistakes. Today artificial intelligence draws better and better. If what opened the conversation was precisely imperfection, could an AI that gets more and more accurate end up, unintentionally, closing it sooner? The underlying question is what we are looking for when we show the community an image: for it to approve a drawing, or for a conversation to open up.
