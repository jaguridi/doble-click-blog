This is Doble Lectura, from Doble Click, and today we talk about officials in a local government in Vietnam who use generative artificial intelligence without rules to protect them.

The study was done in Binh Duong, a province that ranks among the country's most advanced in digital transformation. The authors chose it precisely for that reason: if the obstacles showed up there, they reason, they would probably weigh as much or more in places with fewer resources. They interviewed twenty-five provincial officials in June 2025.

What the interviewees describe is neither formal adoption nor rejection. They use the tool to draft documents and summarize information, and many estimate that it saves them between thirty and fifty percent of their time. That is their perception, not a measurement. Supervisors encourage them, but there are no plans for its use, there is no budget, the tools do not connect with government systems, and the available training is described as basic. Some pay for the subscription out of their own pocket.

The authors call this an accountability vacuum. It brings together three fears: leaking state information, working without clear rules, and not knowing who is answerable if the tool gets it wrong. According to the study, that last one is the central barrier. As one interviewee sums it up, if there are mistakes, the employee takes all the responsibility. The result is shadow use, with personal accounts and often without telling supervisors. From there, the paper interprets the caution of older officials not as fear of technology, but as a rational response to a personal risk with no backing.

Now, the view from Latin America, which is this blog's interpretation. In Colombia, the Office of the Comptroller General of the Republic and the Bogotá Mayor's Office have already written rules that were missing in Vietnam: which data cannot be entered into open tools, and who is answerable for what is produced with the help of artificial intelligence. But both leave that responsibility with the individual, exactly what the study describes as paralyzing. Our hypothesis is that clarity helps, although caution could persist if the rules do not come together with approved tools paid for by the institution. In Chile, a program of the Ministry of Labor and Sofofa offers free artificial intelligence courses to public officials. It would be worth measuring whether that training goes further than the basic courses the Vietnamese interviewees considered insufficient.

It remains open how widely this pattern repeats. The study is qualitative, looks at a single province, and explains a mechanism; it does not measure how widespread it is.
