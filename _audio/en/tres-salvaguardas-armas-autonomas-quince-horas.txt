In early September, the countries gathered in Geneva agreed on a first framework on lethal autonomous weapons, that is, systems that can choose and attack a target without a person pulling the trigger. Now, a report by The Washington Post reconstructs what happened in the final fifteen hours of that negotiation.

According to the paper, in a closed session, with the cameras off and no observers, the delegations of the United States and Russia removed at least three safeguards from the text. The first required these systems to operate predictably and reliably. The second made it mandatory to consider ethical aspects in their use. And the third called for a person to review the targets identified by artificial intelligence before an attack. The report adds that both delegations arrived with about ten lawyers each, almost twice as many as the others.

Now, our reading. The most concrete loss is that third clause, and it is felt by the defense ministries that buy these systems rather than build them. A multilateral text with human review gave them a common standard to require in their contracts. Without it, each buyer will have to negotiate it on its own. There is a less severe reading: the text was never binding, so the deleted clause did not oblige anyone either.

The report does not include the position of any Latin American country in that final session.

What remains to be seen comes in November, when states return to Geneva to decide whether the process moves on to negotiating a binding agreement, now on the basis of a text without those three safeguards.
