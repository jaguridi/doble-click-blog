Doble Lectura, from Doble Click. Today, research from Anthropic on how to read what a model is about to say, before it says it.

The starting idea comes from neuroscience. In people, only a fraction of what the brain processes becomes available for deliberate thought and for putting into words. The rest happens automatically. The question the work asks is whether something functionally similar has emerged in language models, and the answer it gives is yes: models maintain a small set of internal representations available for reporting and reasoning, on top of a much larger volume of processing that runs on its own.

But the real contribution is not the analogy, it is the instrument. The authors build a lens that, at any point in the processing, returns the list of concepts the model is in a position to verbalize there. It is a way of looking at the draft before it becomes an answer.

With that comes the finding that will circulate the most. When the lens was applied to evaluation scenarios, the model's strategic assessments appeared in that internal space even when they were not visible in its answer. And in models that were deliberately given a misaligned disposition, the lens revealed the internal signature of that disposition.

It is worth being very clear about something, because this is where it will get twisted in translation. The authors explicitly state that they work with a purely functional notion and take no position on subjective experience. The work does not claim that models are conscious. It claims that there is an internal organization with similar properties.

From Latin America, there is good news with a catch. The tool is lightweight to operate: it does not require retraining anything, and it produces outputs a person can read, although building it carries an upfront cost, a computation done only once. It is exactly the kind of audit instrument that a university or a ministry team could operate without buying frontier compute. But to apply it you need access to the model's internal states, and only whoever holds the weights, or the lab itself, has that. A state that consumes closed models through an interface cannot use this, nor hire someone to use it.

That is where the question lies. A concrete technique for auditing from the inside appears, and the bottleneck is once again the same as always: who has access to the guts. That reading is mine, not the paper's. If auditing requires opening up the model, what should the region demand at the moment of purchase?

The work is by a team of sixteen people from Anthropic's interpretability group, with Wes Gurnee, Nicholas Sofroniew and Jack Lindsey as lead contributors. It is worth remembering that this is a lab studying its own models, not an independent audit.
