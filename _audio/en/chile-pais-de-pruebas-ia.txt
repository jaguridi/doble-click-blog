Doble Lectura, from Doble Click. Today, how artificial intelligence is understood in Chile, according to those who use it, build it and regulate it.

A team of Chilean researchers, led by Teresa Correa, did something unusual: it brought together in a single study three actors who almost never look at one another. On one side, users of public services that already run on algorithms, such as school admission or medical leave. On another, the developers who program these systems. And third, the authorities who debated in Congress how to regulate artificial intelligence. It is a qualitative study, done with interviews, focus groups and observation of the legislative sessions between 2023 and 2024. It does not seek to measure, but to understand how each group thinks.

The first finding is the most striking. All three groups share the same image: Chile as a testing ground. Users feel like guinea pigs, exposed to technologies that no one explains to them. Developers talk about a sandbox country, where an idea is tested but cannot be scaled, and ends up being exported abroad. And in Congress, industry voices describe the region as a playground, a place of consumption, while the models are developed in the rich world. The authors connect this to an old local memory, that of the country used as a laboratory.

The second finding is subtler, and perhaps the most useful. What people demand of artificial intelligence changes depending on whether they talk about it in the abstract or about a specific system. Faced with the general idea of artificial intelligence, users trust it as long as it works, and ask for little more. But when the focus shifts to a specific system that makes decisions about their health or their children's school, skepticism appears, along with the demand for explanations, and above all for human mediation.

From Latin America, the value is clear: this is evidence from the inside, in Spanish, in real services and institutions. And it leaves a policy lesson. If people only demand transparency when they see the system that affects them, the task is not to discuss artificial intelligence in the abstract, but to make the specific systems visible. The question that remains is that very one: as long as the conversation stays general, who stands up for those who suffer it up close?
