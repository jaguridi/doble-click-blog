Doble Lectura, from Doble Click. Today, why not everything called artificial intelligence regulation really regulates the same thing.

A team from Stanford and Harvard put together a taxonomy, a grid of eleven criteria, to compare in an orderly way the artificial intelligence laws of five places: the European Union, the United States, Canada, China and Brazil. It is not an experiment or a piece about which law is better. It is a tool for avoiding confusion.

And the confusion, they say, starts with a single word. The label artificial intelligence regulation is used equally for voluntary guidelines, with no obligation, and for binding laws with penalties. When a recommendation is presented as if it were a law with teeth, it creates a false sense of security: people believe they are protected by strict rules that do not actually exist. Comparing those two things, as the paper's title says, is comparing apples to oranges.

The grid shows where the five frameworks diverge. Some regulate according to the risk level of each use; others stretch sectoral laws they already had. Some require testing before the system is used; others wait until there is harm. And the most opaque criterion, according to the authors, is who takes part in designing the law. That is where the most uncomfortable part appears: in Canada, of 253 stakeholders consulted, 216 came from the business world. That imbalance is the raw material of regulatory capture, when industry ends up writing the rules it is supposed to follow.

An interesting nuance: the study dismantles the idea that regulating and innovating are mutually exclusive. China, with strict rules, remains a leader in artificial intelligence development.

And from Latin America? Brazil is one of the five cases, not an appendix. Its bill would be the first comprehensive artificial intelligence law in the region if it is passed. What follows is our own reading, not the paper's: most Latin American countries, outside Brazil, are still in the territory of strategies and voluntary principles. And that is where the central warning lands: if a statement of good intentions is communicated as if it were a binding law, the risk is that citizens let their guard down, believing they are protected. The grid is useful precisely for that, for asking each framework what is real and what is facade.

The question it leaves works for any congress in the region. Before celebrating that a country already regulates artificial intelligence, it is worth checking what type of regulation it is, because not everything that carries that name obliges anyone to do anything.

The study is by Sacha Alanoca, Shira Gur-Arieh, Tom Zick and Kevin Klyman. This was an automated reading done by Claude; for the details, it is worth checking the original paper.
