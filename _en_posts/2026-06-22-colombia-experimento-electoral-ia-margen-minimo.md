What changed today: Colombia now has a result, and it is the one no one predicted. Abelardo de la Espriella won the presidential runoff with 49.66% against 48.70% for Iván Cepeda —a margin of less than one point, when polls gave De la Espriella a seven-point lead— with a turnout of 63.6% ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/6/22/colombian-right-wing-candidate-de-la-espriella-wins-tight-presidential-race)). On election day itself, the Technology Ministry (MinTIC) published findings on coordinated digital impersonation campaigns that were still active while the polls were open ([MinTIC](https://mintic.gov.co/portal/inicio/Sala-de-prensa/Noticias/438823:Gobierno-hace-llamado-a-frenar-la-violencia-digital-y-la-desinformacion-de-cara-a-la-segunda-vuelta-presidencial)). We had been following this campaign because it exposed a state capable of detecting disinformation in real time, but not of sanctioning it: Law 2502, which provides the mechanism to punish deepfakes —fake videos or audio fabricated with artificial intelligence—, does not take effect until July 2026.

Such a tight result makes the experiment's underlying question impossible to answer. With six documented fabrications in the campaign and a final margin of 0.96 points, there is still no methodology to estimate how many votes each piece of disinformation moved. The central unknown closes without an answer just as voting ends, and that is precisely what makes the Colombian case so instructive: it is not a model of regulatory success, but the most detailed map the region has of the gap between what a state can see and what it can prevent.

That distinction matters because Colombia does not vote alone in this cycle. Brazil goes to the polls in October 2026, and Chile and Argentina have their own calendars ahead. The legacy Colombia hands over is neither reassuring nor catastrophic: it is concrete. It showed that it is possible to put together electoral monitoring deployed on several fronts and that such monitoring works for observing and warning. And it showed, just as clearly, that detecting without being able to sanction does not stop the erosion of democratic trust; it documents it precisely.

## In the region

In three days, on June 25 and 26, the region's ministers in the field meet in Santo Domingo, and they arrive with the most concrete material available so far for discussing electoral frameworks: a presidential election decided by less than one point in which the state detected disinformation operations in real time but could not apply a single sanction. The discussion stops being abstract. The practical question Colombia puts on the table is whether the region can close the timing gap —the distance between the capacity to detect and the capacity to sanction— before the next major vote, and not after.
