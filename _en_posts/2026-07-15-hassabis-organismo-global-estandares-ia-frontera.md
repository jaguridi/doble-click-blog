Demis Hassabis, CEO of Google DeepMind, published an essay and gave an interview to Axios proposing something no one at his level had put on the table in such detail until now: an independent global standards body for frontier AI. The idea, as [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/14/deepmind-ceo-calls-for-an-independent-standards-body-to-regulate-frontier-ai/), is an entity funded by the industry itself and backed by the US government, modeled on FINRA, the self-regulatory organization that oversees the US financial industry. Labs would submit their models up to 30 days before launch for testing of cyber, biological and "deception" risks.

It matters because it is the most concrete proposal for formal governance of advanced AI to come from one of the three most influential lab CEOs on the planet. In a sector where self-regulation tends to stall at vague principles, Hassabis sketches a mechanism with rules, deadlines and a mandatory prior review. The problem, seen from Latin America, is one of political architecture: a body "funded by the industry and backed by the United States" reproduces the same pattern the region has already experienced firsthand. When the US Department of Commerce used its export controls (the so-called BIS ban) to decide which frontier models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5, the rest of the world could or could not see, those rules were designed entirely between Washington and US labs. No seats reserved for anyone else. Hassabis's proposal, however well-intentioned, risks institutionalizing that asymmetry: whoever certifies which model is "safe" decides, de facto, which AI each country gets access to.
