Today Brazil's Chamber of Deputies votes on [PL 2338/2023](https://murayama.com.br/en/2026/05/18/chamber-of-deputies-schedules-vote-on-artificial-intelligence-legal-framework-for-may-27/), Latin America's first artificial intelligence regulatory framework with full national scope. It reaches the threshold after 18 months in the legislative process: if the floor approves the text without changes, the law is one step away from the presidential signature; if it amends it, it goes back to the Senate and could be left for 2027. It is, without exaggeration, the most important AI policy event the region has ever had.

The bill follows the European model: it classifies systems by risk level, creates a National AI System and provides for fines of up to 50 million reais. The hottest point is the copyright chapter the Senate approved in 2024 and which, according to [more than 50 organizations from the creative sector](https://www2.camara.leg.br/atividade-legislativa/comissoes/comissoes-temporarias/especiais/57a-legislatura/comissao-especial-sobre-inteligencia-artificial-pl-2338-23), rapporteur Aguinaldo Ribeiro's report may have removed. The regional contrast is striking: in Chile, an equivalent article on protected works was rejected in mid-May. The underlying question is whether Latin America can build AI governance that is truly its own or whether it will end up adapting a mold that Europe itself simplified and postponed the same month.

## In the region

It is probably the densest week of AI governance Latin America has ever experienced. While Brazil decides on PL 2338, the European Commission is moving forward with draft classifications of high-risk systems under its AI Act (the same model that inspires the Brazilian text), with public consultation open until June 23. The coincidence matters: whatever Europe ends up fine-tuning will directly affect how the future Brazilian law is interpreted and applied. And the region's absence from the first multilateral data governance cohort, just as Brazil legislates, highlights the distance between global capacity-building processes and the legislative decisions made country by country.

## Threads we're following

Today's vote adds to something we had been following: the tug-of-war between two ways of regulating AI in the region. On one side, Brazil has been pushing for a year and a half a broad framework inspired by Europe; on the other, Chile rejected a key piece on protected works a few weeks ago. That this happens the same month the European Union is simplifying and delaying its own rules makes the question more urgent: does it make sense to copy a model its author is rewriting, or is there room for a path of our own?
