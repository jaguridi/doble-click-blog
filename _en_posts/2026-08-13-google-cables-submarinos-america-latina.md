The most consequential decision for Latin America's artificial intelligence infrastructure this week was made by a company. Google Cloud unveiled [Americas Connect](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/infrastructure/introducing-americas-connect), an expansion of its submarine cable network with three new systems: **Alisios**, which will link the Dominican Republic, Panama, and Chile with redundant routes to Google's data centers in Chile, Los Angeles, and Las Vegas; **Canoa**, from the Dominican Republic to Bermuda, linked to the Nuvem and Sol cables to reach South Carolina, Virginia, and Madrid; and **OlaLuz**, which will connect the Dominican Republic directly with Florida. On top of that comes a branch of the Firmina cable that lands on Dominican territory.

The Caribbean country sits at the center of the design: it would host Google's eighth digital exchange point in the world and the first in Latin America, with completion expected in early 2027 and up to ten times more fiber pairs available. The names are deliberately regional (alisios for the Caribbean trade winds, canoa from Taíno, OlaLuz from the words for wave and light), and the announcement gathers endorsements from the governments of the Dominican Republic, Panama, Bermuda, and Chile, all celebrating something they did not negotiate among themselves.

What was decided here is not where a data center goes, but something that comes before it: where the traffic flows, which country concentrates the redundancy, and therefore which state can even aspire to host compute later. Chile is connected to the Pacific by a route via Panama that it does not control, in parallel with its own bet on the Humboldt cable. The same day, Uruguay responded with the only tool a small state has left: its public telecommunications company [announced $70 million for its own data center](https://www.gub.uy/presidencia/comunicacion/noticias/antel-summit-ente-se-transforma-empresa-tecnologica-dijo-alejandro-paz) in Canelones, with 250 jobs, construction starting in 2027, and data sovereignty as the central argument. Three private cables against a public investment equivalent to a fraction of a single frontier campus.

## In the region

The most important move for Latin America this week is not a law but an invitation. The U.S. State Department announced an artificial intelligence summit in Lima for September 8, within a statement on growth opportunities in the bilateral relationship, as the new Peruvian government deepens its alignment with Washington. The Peruvian digital rights organization [Hiperderecho published an alert](https://hiperderecho.org/2026/08/estados-unidos-anuncia-cumbre-de-ia-en-peru-por-que-debemos-estar-alertas/) with an argument that applies to the whole region: the diplomatic and technology agenda is moving faster than democratic controls. Its focus is on Palantir, whose platforms integrate large volumes of data from different sources and turn them into maps, profiles, alerts, and operational decisions. The precedent it cites has verifiable figures: Ecuador hired Palantir in May 2025 for its customs authority SENAE for an initial $6.6 million, later expanded by about $17.2 million, and within 100 days the platform was profiling more than 90% of customs declarations by integrating data from several institutions. Hiperderecho's request is deliberately modest and for that very reason hard to reject: publish before the event the agenda, the participating companies, the invited public entities, the sponsorships, and any proposed contract or pilot. It is the minimum standard that no country in the region applies today to technology summits with foreign governments, and there are three weeks to test whether it is met. In parallel, ChatGPT advertising arriving in Mexico and Brazil leaves the data protection authorities of both countries with a new problem on their desks: the targeting uses the full conversation history, something neither of them has evaluated yet.
