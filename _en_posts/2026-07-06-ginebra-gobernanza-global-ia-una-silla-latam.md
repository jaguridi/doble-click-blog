This Monday, the [first UN Global Dialogue on Artificial Intelligence Governance](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/un-global-dialogue-opens-urgent-call-safe-and-inclusive-ai-benefits-all) opened in Geneva, the first intergovernmental forum in history devoted to this issue, with all 193 member states at the table alongside the private sector, academia and civil society. Secretary-General António Guterres called for governing the technological transformation "before being governed by it" and proposed a global child protection pact: systems verified as safe for minors before their launch, zero tolerance for AI-generated child sexual abuse, and referral to human help when there are signs of distress.

In the plenary session, the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, co-chaired by Yoshua Bengio and Nobel Peace Prize laureate Maria Ressa, delivered its central warning: current science cannot guarantee that the growth in model capabilities will not end up causing "catastrophic harm." For Latin America the meeting matters twice over. As a milestone, because it is the first time AI governance is discussed in a space with universal participation. And as a mirror of an asymmetry: the region has a single formal leadership seat (El Salvador co-chairs the dialogue together with Estonia) and a single direct scientific voice, that of Chilean Loreto Bravo, of the Universidad del Desarrollo, [one of three Latin Americans among the panel's 40 experts](https://www.udd.cl/noticias/2026/07/03/academica-udd-expondra-en-el-primer-dialogo-global-sobre-gobernanza-de-la-inteligencia-artificial-de-naciones-unidas-en-ginebra/). Bravo warned that AI's productivity gains "are not automatic" without infrastructure and institutional capacity. Hours before the opening, no government in the region had confirmed a ministerial delegation of its own, unlike India, which sent one headed by its Minister of State for External Affairs.

## In the region

The Geneva dialogue brings into view two paths the region is traveling at once. In multilateral forums, Latin American representation is thin: one country co-chairing and three experts on the scientific panel. In domestic regulation, Argentina and Mexico mark the two ends of the spectrum. Argentina is moving forward, amid controversy, toward a legal form for AI-run companies that almost no country has; Mexico, by contrast, documents its own paralysis with hard data (two thirds of its bills stalled) just as it faces midterm elections exposed to bots and synthetic content. It is the same question seen from two angles: where a country's capacity to govern the AI already operating within its borders is really defined.
