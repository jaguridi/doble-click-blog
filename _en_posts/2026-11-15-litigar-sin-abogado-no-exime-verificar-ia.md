## In 60 seconds

- **What happened.** The Supreme Court of Puerto Rico fined two siblings who were litigating without a lawyer for filing briefs with citations falsely attributed to that court.
- **Why it matters.** Anyone who appears on their own is now on notice that they must review what they draft with AI with care similar to what is required of a lawyer.
- **What we don't know yet.** Whether Puerto Rico's Judicial Branch will offer guidance to people who litigate on their own, beyond warning them that they can be sanctioned for false citations.

Seven documents with dozens of nonexistent citations reached three levels of Puerto Rico's courts: the Court of First Instance, the Court of Appeals and the Supreme Court itself. They were filed by Víctor Manuel and Mercedes Velázquez Feliciano, in an inheritance dispute. The count appears in the separate opinion of Justice Raúl Candelario López, joined by three other justices, within [resolution 2026 TSPR 110](https://dts.poderjudicial.pr/ts/2026/2026tspr110.pdf). The case is CC-2026-0815, and the fine must be paid within 30 days. Infobae reported it on November 14.

The court rejected the siblings' appeal and set a standard for those who appear pro se, that is, without legal representation. They must validate AI-generated content, and failing in that duty can bring financial or other sanctions. The same opinion clarifies that using AI is not, by itself, grounds for a sanction. The sanction fell on the citations falsely attributed to the court. According to Candelario López, it is the first time the Supreme Court has ruled on the issue for litigants without a lawyer.

For Puerto Rico's trial and appellate judges, the practical effect could be immediate. Faced with a brief containing invented citations, they now have a Supreme Court standard for imposing sanctions without waiting for an additional rule. For those who litigate on their own, the resolution suggests that checking every citation delivered by an AI assistant becomes their responsibility.
