When OpenAI unveiled "Presence" on July 22 (a platform for companies to deploy public-facing voice and chat agents), the first reading was that Latin America was left out: limited access and English only. Reports from July 23 correct that idea. Among the pilot customers is [BBVA México, alongside SoftBank and IAG](https://openai.com/index/introducing-openai-presence/), exploring voice support for customer service. It is the first concrete and verifiable case of a leading bank in the region adopting a frontier lab's voice agents to speak directly with its customers.

What matters is not only the speed but the type of deployment: it is not an internal productivity pilot but a public-facing channel, where an AI answers on the bank's behalf. That shifts the debate from "when will agentic AI reach banking in the region" to another, more uncomfortable one: what standards of auditing, accountability and quality of service the region will demand of an agent that is already operating. And it comes at a time when neither the central banks nor the financial regulators of Latin America have specific frameworks for evaluating third-party conversational agents. Adoption is running ahead of the rules.

## In the region

The regional backdrop is one of dependence. Much of Latin America's cheap access to frontier AI today goes through Chinese open-weights models such as Kimi, Qwen and GLM. That is why it matters that the US Treasury is threatening sanctions against Moonshot AI, the creator of Kimi, over an alleged distillation of an Anthropic model, an accusation whose technical premise some independent experts have already begun to question. If the sanctions regime advances in the bilateral negotiation scheduled for September, the region could lose that access route without ever having had a seat at the table. Meanwhile, adoption is not waiting: a [study in Chile](https://www.publimetro.cl/tecnologia/2026/07/23/en-chile-8-de-cada-10-empresas-ya-usan-inteligencia-artificial-pero-la-ciberseguridad-emerge-como-el-principal-desafio/) found that 8 in 10 companies already use artificial intelligence and that cybersecurity is their main challenge. The pattern repeats: the technology is being used faster than anyone knows how to govern it.
