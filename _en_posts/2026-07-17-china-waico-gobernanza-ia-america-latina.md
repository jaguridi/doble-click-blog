This week the geopolitics of artificial intelligence gained a second protagonist. On July 16 in Shanghai, China formalized the founding of the World AI Cooperation Organization (WAICO), with 29 founding countries, among them Brazil, Cuba and Venezuela, and even UN Secretary-General António Guterres in attendance. The next day, in his first in-person keynote at the World AI Conference in Shanghai, [Xi Jinping offered explicit AI cooperation to CELAC](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-17/xi-vows-to-make-ai-for-all-in-debut-at-china-s-top-tech-summit), the bloc of Latin American and Caribbean countries, and promised 5,000 training slots for developing nations.

What makes this the story of the day is not the announcement itself but the map it draws. In the same week that Demis Hassabis is promoting in Washington a standards body for frontier AI modeled on the US financial regulator, a parallel governance bloc is emerging, this one led by China, openly courting Latin America. And there lies the uncomfortable detail: neither of the two included the region in its original design, but both are seeking it out now that it is already built. For Latin American governments, the decision stops being technical and becomes strategic: which table do you sit at when none was built with you in mind?

## In the region

The picture of the week is two AI governance blocs competing to define the global rules, with Latin America in the middle, having sat at the design table of neither. On one side, the financial-regulator-style standards body that Hassabis is pushing from Washington; on the other, the WAICO newly founded by China, which already counts Brazil, Cuba and Venezuela among its founding members. In parallel, several countries are advancing on their own path: [Colombia is debating an AI bill in committee](https://www.sercolombiano.com/2026/07/16/colombia-avanza-en-la-regulacion-de-la-inteligencia-artificial-y-se-posiciona-entre-los-paises-lideres-de-america-latina/), still without a confirmed legislative milestone, which would place it in the region's regulatory "second wave" alongside Brazil, Chile and Mexico. The underlying question is whether the region ends up choosing a bloc or building its own.
