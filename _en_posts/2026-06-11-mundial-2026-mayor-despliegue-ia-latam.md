The 2026 World Cup kicked off today at Estadio Azteca with Mexico vs. South Africa, and with the opening whistle the largest mass deployment of artificial intelligence in Latin America's history was switched on. Google launched its [Gemini features for the tournament](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/fifa-world-cup-google-tools-2026/) —real-time scores on the lock screen, AI-generated sports summaries and photo templates with national team jerseys— and also confirmed that Argentina's national team uses Gemini for tactical analysis and injury prevention. With Android on more than 80% of devices in the region, the World Cup turns hundreds of millions of Latin American phones into de facto Gemini terminals, without most users actively choosing it.

But the same country hosting the opening match also deployed "Nexus," a facial recognition and vehicle tracking platform for the event's logistics hubs, with no announced date for its withdrawal. These are two layers of the same tournament: consumer AI, visible and friendly on the phone screen, and surveillance AI, invisible at the stadium gate. Google describes its features as "a tool to connect fans with the game they love"; state facial recognition runs in parallel, without that friendly narrative and without a timetable for being switched off. For the region, the World Cup thus works as an enormous laboratory where the two faces of the same technology are tested at the same time and on the same crowd.

## In the region

On June 10 the European Union published its [final Code of Practice on labeling AI-generated content](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_1328), the first operational technical standard —based on the C2PA standard, which adds a mark of origin to files— that requires *deepfakes* in content of public interest to be identified. It takes effect on August 2. The underlying pattern is that three very different AI governance models now coexist —the voluntary approach of the United States, the European obligations and this new labeling code— and none was designed with Latin American participation or is binding on its territory. Colombia is the most exposed case: it reaches the June 21 presidential runoff without an equivalent rule in force.

## Threads we're following

For weeks we have been following Colombia's presidential election as the acid test of AI disinformation in the region. Today another chapter was added: ten days before the runoff, the National Registrar [warned about the violence that could result from AI-generated disinformation](https://www.eltiempo.com/politica/elecciones-colombia-2026/elecciones-presidenciales-en-colombia-2026-registrador-nacional-manifesto-preocupacion-por-violencia-derivada-de-la-desinformacion-en-segunda-vuelta-3562970). There are already four documented *deepfakes*, and cloned audio is available for about $10 a month, while the Colombian law that should punish them still has no effective sanctions. It is the biggest regulatory asymmetry in electoral AI we have seen in the region: the technology to fake is cheap and abundant; the rule to stop it still has no teeth.
