Today Colombia holds the first round of its presidential elections: 41.4 million eligible voters, 14 candidacies and, according to the Electoral Observation Mission (MOE), active circulation of candidate deepfakes, cloned audio and fake polls fabricated with artificial intelligence. All of this is happening without the country having an AI legal framework or specific electoral regulation for this type of content ([NoticiasSuper](https://www.noticiassuper.com/2026/05/29/elecciones-presidenciales-en-colombia-el-domingo-este-alerta-a-deepfakes-noticias-encuestas-y-videos-falsos-generados-con-ia-y-a-otras-amenazas-para-la-democracia-en-la-era-digital/)).

The most talked-about case is a fake poll that showed one candidate winning and that circulated on WhatsApp: [El Tiempo](https://www.eltiempo.com/politica/elecciones-colombia-2026/elfiltro-sondeo-en-el-que-ivan-cepeda-ganaria-en-primera-vuelta-la-presidencia-de-colombia-fue-hecho-con-ia-3554932) identified it as AI-generated because it still had the Gemini logo visible in the lower right corner. It is a revealing detail: the piece of disinformation did not even bother to remove the mark of the tool that created it. The MOE sums up the underlying shift this way: "the central challenge is no longer the physical tampering of votes, but the systematic deception of voters." To gauge the terrain, the organization itself estimates that 78% of Colombians were exposed to digital disinformation in the past year.

The regional contrast is what makes this Sunday so interesting. Just four days earlier, Brazil approved its first comprehensive national AI framework, and its electoral court already operates with specific measures against synthetic content. Colombia goes to the polls with none of those instruments. If no candidate gets more than 50% plus one, there will be a runoff on June 21, and then the practical question will be whether it is possible to build any institutional response in just three weeks.

## In the region

The week that is ending leaves Latin America with its densest regulatory map on AI to date, and at the same time exposes its unevenness. Brazil approved a national framework with a risk-based approach and fines that can reach 50 million reais, added decrees against "nudifiers" and on platform liability, and maintains specific electoral rules. Colombia, in the same week, votes with deepfakes circulating in real time and without an equivalent institutional tool. Big tech (Meta, OpenAI, Google, TikTok) has already voiced its rejection of the Brazilian decrees, citing "legal uncertainty," a tension that will probably repeat itself when Chile, Argentina or other countries in the region move forward with similar rules. The debate, at bottom, is about who has the authority and the speed to respond when technology runs faster than the law.
