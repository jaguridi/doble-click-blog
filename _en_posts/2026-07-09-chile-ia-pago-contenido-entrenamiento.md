From Geneva, in the middle of the UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance, Chilean Minister Ximena Lincolao took a stance on a dispute brewing in her own country: "none of our initiatives contemplates including Article 8," the provision that would have exempted artificial intelligence platforms from paying to use protected content (books, music, news, art) to train their models, [as reported by Grupo de Diarios América](https://gda.com/detalle-de-la-noticia/?article=6121679). The statement did not come on just any day: it coincided with the expiration of the deadline for amendments in the Senate Finance Committee for the sweeping reform bill that includes, among other things, that exemption.

The detail matters because it defines who bears the cost of training an AI. "Article 8" would have allowed a model to learn from copyrighted works without compensating their creators, on the argument of enabling a local artificial intelligence ecosystem. Dropping it tips the balance toward authors, but it raises the underlying question: can a small country build its own AI capabilities if it must also pay licenses for all the training material? It is a dilemma no government in the region has yet resolved, which is why the Chilean case works as a trial run watched from outside. That the minister chose a multilateral forum to settle a domestic debate, on the very day of her legislative deadline, shows how much global and local policy intersect today when it comes to AI.
