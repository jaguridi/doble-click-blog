Nine days before the presidential runoff, Colombia's digital campaign already has a formal diagnosis: the Electoral Observation Mission (MOE) presented [three warnings about the online debate ahead of the June 21 runoff](https://www.eltiempo.com/politica/las-tres-alertas-de-la-moe-sobre-la-campana-digital-de-cara-a-la-segunda-vuelta-presidencial-amenazas-ia-y-plata-sin-rastrear-3563360). This is no longer a general concern about *deepfakes*: it is a report with data, which sorts into three fronts what we had been following as a single diffuse threat.

The first is the escalation of hostility. After the May 31 first round, the monitoring —carried out together with UNDP and the Foundation for Press Freedom— found that the conversation moved from insults to "explicit references to physical violence, direct threats and calls to silence or eliminate" political opponents. The second is disinformation with artificial intelligence: pieces fabricated and distributed with AI, added to rumors of fraud without evidence, which erode fact-based deliberation. The third is the newest in the story: untraceable money. As of May 31, fourteen campaigns reported more than 99.5 billion pesos in income and 85.1 billion in spending, but the MOE was unable to reconcile much of the digital advertising with the declared amounts. In other words: political ads circulating without anyone being able to know clearly who pays for them. Three different problems, a single terrain —social media— and a single deadline: June 21, when Abelardo de la Espriella and Iván Cepeda face each other at the polls.

## In the region

The MOE report does not happen in a regulatory vacuum by chance. While Colombia reaches the runoff without a rule in force requiring AI-fabricated content to be identified, other jurisdictions are tightening the calendar: the European Union will require [labeling of *deepfakes* of public interest starting August 2](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_1328), and the bulk of its AI regulation begins to apply that same day, with fines of up to 35 million euros. The asymmetry is the usual one seen from the region: the technology to fake is cheap and available today; the rule to stop it arrives late, and when it arrives, it is designed elsewhere. Colombia is, these weeks, the most exposed case of that gap.
