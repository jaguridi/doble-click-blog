The artificial intelligence surveillance infrastructure of an entire Latin American country costs $1.2 million, and that is the news. [Amnesty International published "Estado de vigilancia" ("Surveillance State")](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/08/argentina-unchecked-deployment-of-ai-driven-surveillance-reinforces-a-techno-authoritarian-infrastructure-of-social-control/), the first report to reconstruct the Argentine state's technological inventory for mass surveillance using only public procurement documents and freedom of information requests. There was no leak and no anonymous source: the record was in plain sight.

What the state bought between 2024 and 2025 reads like a catalog: open-source intelligence platforms (OSINT, systems that collect and cross-reference large volumes of data from social networks, websites, the dark web, and technical infrastructure), facial recognition licenses, drones and ground control stations with automated tracking, thermal cameras, and real-time image transmission equipment. The methodology combines twenty-one interviews with journalists, activists, retirees, and human rights defenders with the analysis of dozens of procurement files. Matt Mahmoudi, the organization's adviser on AI and human rights, sums up the finding: these technologies "have become an integral part of the infrastructure of state control."

What is relevant for the region is not Argentina: it is the method. Public procurement is becoming the most realistic point of control Latin America has over state AI, far ahead of any law, because it is where capability becomes a contract before it becomes policy. It is acquired for amounts that do not require a major tender, from vendors that do not announce themselves, and the only public trace is audited by whoever decides to sit down and read the purchase orders. The question the report leaves is uncomfortable in its simplicity: what would the same exercise find in ChileCompra, in SECOP II, in ComprasNet, or in SEACE.

## In the region

The day's second regional story comes from Brazil, in the middle of the campaign: [Aos Fatos's analysis of the government programs of the five main presidential candidates](https://www.aosfatos.org/noticias/incentivo-data-centers-programas-governo-presidenciaveis/) shows that attracting AI data centers is the only point on which they all agree. The differences are of emphasis (Lula makes it conditional on energy efficiency and water impact assessment, Renan Santos proposes turning the Northeast into the "computational Arabia"), but none of the five programs sets verifiable conditions in return: no audited electricity rate, no access to compute for public universities, no minimum employment, in hyperscale facilities that employ between 30 and 50 people. The contrast with the executive order Pennsylvania signed this week (prior municipal approval, a ban on confidentiality agreements, the operator pays for the full power grid) is exact: there the template is one of conditions, here it is one of incentives. Brazil is going to receive that infrastructure no matter who governs; what is still open is under what contract.
