Tomorrow, June 25, UNESCO's Third Ministerial Summit on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence in Latin America and the Caribbean opens in Santo Domingo, organized together with the Dominican Republic's OGTIC and the development bank CAF ([OGTIC](https://ogtic.gob.do)). It is the first regional ministerial forum after a month in which the major AI governance decisions were made without the region in the room: the US ban on the Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models (June 12), a G7 in Évian that closed without a common AI framework (June 17) and a Colombian election decided by less than one point with active digital interference on election day (June 21).

The ministers of technology and digitalization arrive with two unprecedented facts on the table that were not on the original agenda, and with just 48 hours to produce a declaration, scheduled for June 26. The underlying question is whether that document will name what the region lived through this month —or whether it will repeat the principles that have been circulating for three years without a mechanism to enforce them. The summit cannot reverse any of those three facts, but it could set what minimum standard the region would commit to having operational before Brazil's elections in October 2026, and what consultation channel it would demand in the face of the next "off switch" decided in Washington ([UNESCO](https://unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence)).

The technical backdrop arrived at almost the same time. Google DeepMind published a [roadmap for controlling AI agents](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/securing-the-future-of-ai-agents/) with fifteen "defense in depth" controls, built on a principle that clashes with the usual discourse of accelerated adoption: AI agents themselves should be treated as an insider threat, with the same safeguards that would apply to a high-privilege outside contractor. The document sets, in practice, what "deploying AI responsibly" means in 2026 —a standard considerably more demanding than any public framework in force in the region. If a Latin American public institution wanted to use frontier models in an audited and sovereign way, that is the architecture it would need to understand; the uncomfortable question is how many of those fifteen controls it could implement in the next twelve months.

## In the region

Santo Domingo is the first test of whether Latin American multilateralism can produce concrete instruments and not just declarations of principles. The structural risk emerging this week is new: if the way out of the Fable 5 ban requires verifying US residency for a first tier of access, access to frontier models differentiated by citizenship could become the norm rather than the exception. It would be the first precedent of an AI export control applied not to chips or hardware, but to inference itself, with discrimination by nationality built in. For the ministers, that is the most important governance question of the week: not whether the United States can control its exports, but whether the region has any mechanism to demand access conditions equivalent to those of a US citizen.
