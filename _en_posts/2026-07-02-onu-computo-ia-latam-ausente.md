UN Secretary-General António Guterres, together with Yoshua Bengio and Nobel laureate Maria Ressa, presented the [preliminary report of the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI](https://www.un.org/independent-international-scientific-panel-ai/en/preliminary-report), a group of 40 experts whose secretariat is shared by UNESCO and the International Telecommunication Union. Its central conclusion is twofold: current safeguards are not keeping pace with the growth of AI capabilities, and the power to build these systems is extraordinarily concentrated. The United States controls about 75% and China around 15% of the compute (the processing capacity) that trains the world's most advanced models. Together they account for 90%.

In that snapshot of power, no Latin American country appears. For the region, the figure matters beyond the statistic: it is the first time a multilateral scientific panel, backed by the UN, has put a number on a pattern that had been sensed. The report feeds into the first [Global Dialogue on AI Governance](https://www.un.org/independent-international-scientific-panel-ai/en/preliminary-report), held in Geneva on July 6 and 7 alongside the ITU's AI for Good summit. The uncomfortable question is what the region brings to that table when the infrastructure that gives a voice in these discussions is almost entirely in another hemisphere.

Mexico's attempted answer came this same week. The government [presented 13 technological sovereignty projects to its Congress](https://www.jornada.com.mx/noticia/2026/07/01/politica/mexico-apuesta-por-la-soberania-tecnologica-con-13-proyectos-olinia-y-la-ia-ejes-del-plan), with the Coatlicue supercomputer as the centerpiece: 314 petaFLOPS, 14,480 GPUs and an investment of close to 6 billion pesos. It is the most detailed compute sovereignty package a country in the region has brought before a legislature. By contrast, a column drawing on MIT data points out that only 23% of Latin American organizations capture any economic value from AI, compared with 39% globally: building infrastructure is only half the challenge.

## In the region

The week brings together three layers of the same problem. At the top, the UN report quantifies for the first time, with scientific weight, Latin America's absence from the compute map. At the national level, Mexico formalizes before its Congress the region's most ambitious technological sovereignty bet, while Brazil takes a step that is more symbolic than substantive with its new Parliamentary Front on AI. And underneath, the adoption gap persists: fewer than one in four organizations in the region turns AI into economic value. The regional conversation is no longer only about rules and is starting to be, also, about who has the machines.

## Threads we're following

The UN's diagnosis does not arrive in a vacuum. In recent weeks we have been following how Latin America is left on the sidelines of global AI governance decisions that directly affect it: first with the export ban that took the Fable 5 model out of circulation for almost three weeks, without the region taking part in either the shutdown or the reopening. The report presented at the UN is the chapter that puts all those episodes in context: these are not isolated cases but a pattern of concentration of compute power that a scientific panel has just documented with figures.
