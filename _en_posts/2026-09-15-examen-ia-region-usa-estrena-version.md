Today UNESCO [released three new tools](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-launches-new-tools-reinforce-ethical-ai-governance-global-forum-riyadh-saudi-arabia) and, with them, the first aggregate results of the only artificial intelligence readiness assessment that Latin America uses on a large scale. There are three: RAM 2.0, an updated version of the Readiness Assessment Methodology (the questionnaire a country uses to measure how ready it is to adopt and govern AI), which now explicitly incorporates input from women, people with disabilities and Indigenous peoples gathered from more than 20,000 stakeholders; the first global analysis of governance trends, built on more than 55 country assessments already completed; and a toolkit for governments to measure and reduce the environmental footprint of their AI systems. The announcement comes in the week of the 4th Global Forum on the Ethics of AI, which is meeting in Riyadh through Thursday with delegations from 194 countries, and which yesterday we described as the only global forum whose rules the region helped write.

The numbers map it out well: 58 countries have completed the RAM and 77 are receiving support to design national strategies. Among the five cases that UNESCO itself highlights as countries that turned the assessment into policy is Colombia, together with Bangladesh, Ghana, Nigeria and Zimbabwe. What is interesting is not the certificate but the question that can only now be asked with data: launching version 2.0 at the same time as the analysis of the first 55 reports makes it possible to compare, country by country, how many of the recommendations the RAM left in writing in Chile, Brazil or Mexico were carried out and how many remained a statement of good intentions. The environmental toolkit is also the piece most immediately applicable to the region's real profile: countries that host data centers and do not train models, and that until now had no public methodology for demanding accountability on energy consumption.

Two caveats are worth noting. UNESCO's note is dated September 9 and updated on the 11th (it is an announcement ahead of the forum), and there is still no downloadable PDF for any of the three tools: for now there is only [the methodology's official page](https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en/ram). And it is worth looking at who the host is, because that shapes which topics are comfortable to discuss: the ethics forum is co-organized and funded by the Saudi Data and AI Authority and the ICAIRE center, that is, a state that is at the same time one of the world's largest buyers of compute.

## In the region

The regional event of the day was a presidential statement, not a regulation. Claudia Sheinbaum said at her morning press conference that regulating artificial intelligence and social media in campaigns is [a pending discussion](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/nacion/regulacion-de-redes-sociales-e-ia-discusion-pendiente-no-se-si-todavia-se-pueda-por-proceso-electoral-dice-sheinbaum/): she focused on platforms used as a vehicle for paid campaigning and on bot networks, and acknowledged that with the electoral process already underway she does not know whether Congress will have time to take it up ("the electoral period has already begun; I don't know if it can still be done at this point"); she will consult her legal counsel's office. ## Threads we're following

This week we have been keeping count of how many different architectures are being built at the same time to govern the same technology: the multilateral forum in Riyadh, the private standards body that three U.S. labs have been negotiating since July, and the open-source community that China offered the Global South at the BRICS summit. Today a fourth appears, and it is the fastest of all: a monarchy with no regulatory competition managed in two days to bring Nvidia, DeepMind, OpenAI and Anthropic to the same table, something that took a multilateral organization five years. With a difference that matters for the region: of the four, Riyadh's is the only one that produces a document a Latin American country can cite, apply and use to demand accountability.

A background figure to gauge the urgency: Epoch AI, in a measurement with Ipsos, estimates that intensive use of artificial intelligence in the United States went from 8 to 19 percent of adult users between March and August, counting those who use it at least six days a week. There is no equivalent series for Latin America with that frequency, and that absence is also a diagnosis.
