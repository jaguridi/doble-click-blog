OpenAI published [its global ChatGPT usage report](https://openai.com/index/how-the-world-is-putting-chatgpt-to-work/) with data from the second quarter of 2026, and the regional finding is more specific than one might expect: the three countries that climbed the most positions in the ranking of messages per capita are Peru, Uruguay and Costa Rica. They are not Latin America's large markets, but three small, middle-income economies. The report covers 126 countries and notes that parts of Latin America, Oceania and Africa grew faster than the rest of the world.

Two figures help show what people are doing with the tool. In work conversations, people are more than twice as likely to ask for a task to be carried out (writing, editing, programming, analyzing data) as they are outside work. And the fastest-growing use case is generating and analyzing multimedia: 7.8% of messages globally, and more than 10% in Brazil and Colombia. The methodology is stated: about 300,000 messages a month between July 2024 and June 2026, classified automatically on de-identified data and normalized by population using World Bank estimates. The limitation is also stated: the figures are published by the company being measured, without external audit and without raw data. They work as a signal, not as official statistics. The same day, OpenAI removed the cap on text chats in its free plan, a change that mechanically reinforces the trend the report describes.

The timing matters because it comes two days after the [World Bank's World Development Report 2026](https://www.worldbank.org/en/publication/wdr2026), devoted to artificial intelligence and developing economies. That report measures 4.5% of jobs at risk from generative AI in low- and middle-income countries, compared with 14.2% in high-income ones, and 16.2% of jobs with potential for improvement, compared with 18.7%. In other words: less to lose and almost as much to gain, but all of it conditional on closing gaps in electricity, connectivity, skills and institutions. Its director, Gaurav Nayyar, summed up the point in the [official press release](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2026/08/04/ai-offers-lifeline-to-developing-economies-in-an-era-of-weak-growth): "the window to get this right is narrow." A caveat is in order: the press release does not break down figures for Latin America and the Caribbean, so before citing it as regional evidence you have to go to the body of the report. High adoption, pending infrastructure. Usage is measured by the company that sells the product; the gap is measured by a multilateral body. Neither measurement is made by the region.

## In the region

The most concrete institutional move came from Brazil. Portaria TSE nº 463/2026 sets August 16 as the deadline for platforms with more than 5 million users in the country (individually or as an economic group) to submit electoral compliance plans, and for the first time in the region the requirement explicitly includes that generative AI providers describe the technical safeguards that prevent their systems from recommending candidates or producing non-consensual sexual material of candidates, as well as the mechanisms for identifying and labeling synthetic content. [Laís Martins's analysis in Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/brazils-electoral-court-wants-platform-compliance-plans-can-it-enforce-them/) points to the issue that decides everything: the plans have a public layer and a restricted one, and it is not clear that an electoral court has the technical capacity to check what is declared against what the system actually does. The contrast with Colombia, which reached its June runoff without an equivalent institution and with Law 2502 lacking active sanctions, remains the best argument in favor of the Brazilian mechanism; August 16 will show whether paper turns into enforcement. No multilateral body with a regional mandate published anything in this edition's window. Meanwhile, two decisions made abroad affect the region without consulting it: OpenAI partnered with the American Psychological Association to define how ChatGPT should behave with teenagers ages 13 to 17 (the product is the same one Latin American teenagers use, and no professional society from the region is at that table), and Suno announced watermarking and fingerprinting for its generated music, without publishing the technical specification that a radio station, a court or an electoral authority would need in order to verify anything.
