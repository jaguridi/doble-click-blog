## In 60 seconds

- **What happened.** On October 18, Anthropic announced a public benefit fund that would receive 5% of its shares if its shareholders approve it.
- **Why it matters.** Civil society organizations and workers affected by AI could receive resources, although it is not yet known who decides how they are distributed or on what criteria.
- **What we don't know yet.** The company says that in the coming months it will publish the fund's governance structure and the valuation milestones that add shares to it.

The fund is designed to grow with the company. For every 50% increase in Anthropic's value over a baseline that has not been made public, the board may add 2% of the shares to it. That is how [Anthropic's announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/public-benefit-fund) lays it out. It will be a limited liability company wholly owned by the company, not a charity registered as a 501(c)(3). Anthropic says it chose that format so it can switch between different ways of giving, and that the fund's shares will not be used for lobbying or political contributions.

The uses it mentions range from donations to nonprofit organizations to direct payments to specific populations. The example it gives is AI's impact on employment, an issue to which it has already committed $200 million. Together with prior commitments from founders and employees, about 20% of its shares would be dedicated to the public good, according to the company. All of these figures come from the company's announcement.

To pay workers affected by AI directly, someone will have to determine who falls into that category, and that criterion has not been published. Until the structure is known, a union or an organization seeking funds would have no one to turn to and no rules to invoke. External oversight could depend largely on a public benefit report, which Anthropic announced without a date.

## In the region

In Paraguay, the digital rights organization TEDIC documented AI-altered audio and video targeting women city council members in the Central department, ahead of the 2026 municipal elections. According to [its October 14 analysis](https://www.tedic.org/elecciones-y-tecnologia-en-las-municipales-2026/), Law 6971, the latest electoral reform, did not include technology issues. TEDIC proposes requiring, by 2028, that electoral content generated or altered with AI be labeled.

In Chile, 102 students took part in [a study by the CENIA center and the University of Chile](https://cenia.cl/2026/10/16/universitarios-que-conversan-con-chatgpt-creen-entender-mas-de-lo-que-logran-explicar-segun-estudio-de-investigador-cenia/), released on October 16. Those who used ChatGPT averaged 2.47 out of 4 when explaining the texts, compared with 2.75 for those who only read them.
