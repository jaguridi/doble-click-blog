In fourteen days, the US government went from sanctioning an artificial intelligence model to controlling one before it exists for the public. The White House —through its cybersecurity and science policy offices— asked OpenAI to restrict access to its next model, GPT-5.6, to a group of enterprise partners previously approved by the government itself, ahead of the general launch. Sam Altman told employees at an internal meeting on June 25; there is no official statement from either the government or the company, and the information came from internal sources ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/06/25/trump-administration-openai-gpt-model-release)).

It is the first known case of a *preventive* government restriction on a frontier model —the most advanced systems on the market. The difference from what happened at the beginning of the month is not minor. The ban on Fable 5, Anthropic's model, was a reactive sanction: it came after the launch and was justified by a specific security flaw. The GPT-5.6 case is something else: a prior approval mechanism, with no documented incident and no public announcement. If the pattern takes hold, Washington would not be responding to specific threats, but managing in advance which models reach which markets and under what conditions. And for Latin America —which has no seat in either of the two conversations— the practical result is identical in both cases: two of the most capable models available today are out of reach without consultation or prior notice to the region.

On the same day this restriction became known, the region's technology ministers were wrapping up a summit in Santo Domingo that was trying, precisely, to build a common position. The coincidence defines the underlying problem: the region is agreeing on principles about AI ethics while the decisions that determine which tools it can access are made at another table, without it.

## In the region

UNESCO's Third Ministerial Summit on the Ethics of AI in Latin America and the Caribbean closed today in Santo Domingo with more concrete results than its predecessors. Where Santiago (2023) and Montevideo (2024) left declarations of principles, this summit adopted the **Santo Domingo Declaration** and a **Regional Roadmap 2026-2027** with five priority areas and a twelve-month horizon, prepared by CAF and UNESCO with the collaboration of more than twenty countries ([FORO IA LAC](https://foroialac.org/en/cumbres/) · [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence)). The Dominican Republic took over the pro tempore presidency and went a step further: it announced an AI Code of Ethics by presidential decree, the first national instrument adopted directly from the regional multilateral framework.

None of this is enough, on its own, to respond to a unilateral control like the one on GPT-5.6 or the ban on Fable 5; no regional instrument can reverse those decisions today. But it is the first operational AI governance roadmap the region has given itself, and it arrives in the exact week when the distance between agreeing on principles and being able to carry them out became impossible to ignore.
