After more than a decade of fruitless meetings, the 128 States Parties to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons closed by consensus in Geneva [the first global document on lethal autonomous weapons](https://www.reuters.com/world/states-reach-agreement-autonomous-weapons-talks-geneva-2026-09-05/). It is the first time there is a shared definition of that category (systems capable of selecting and attacking a target without a person pulling the trigger) together with a framework for what it means to maintain human control over them. The text is not binding, and that is where it gets interesting.

The human control framework they agreed on requires oversight by a responsible chain of command; operational limits on scale, type of target, time and space; exclusively human authority over the target and the mission; and the ability to deactivate the system. What it does not require is compliance: the United States and Russia, with support from India and Turkey, blocked any mandatory rules and argued for national guidelines, and Washington pushed specifically for flexibility in the human judgment clauses. In the opposite direction, a joint statement led by Brazil and signed by 39 countries called for opening negotiations on a treaty. According to [the organizations that pushed the process for three years](https://www.hrw.org/news/2026/09/04/convention-on-conventional-weapons-group-of-governmental-experts-on-lethal), the document was watered down in the final hours of discussion. The next date has a binary outcome: from November 16 to 20, the Seventh Review Conference decides whether to open negotiations on a treaty, extend the discussion, or simply let the process drop.

The regional angle is worth pausing on, because it is unusual. Of all the artificial intelligence governance files on the international agenda, this is the only one where Latin America does not arrive late or as a spectator. The region has had its own doctrine since 2021, when Brazil, Mexico and Chile jointly warned that without meaningful human control these systems would violate the principle of human dignity, and this time one of those countries led the coalition calling for a treaty. The contrast with the same week is stark: in Geneva the region leads on weapons, and at the G20 it accepts a light-touch regulatory framework written in Washington for civilian artificial intelligence, when the underlying technical problem (delegating irreversible decisions to a system without effective human oversight) is exactly the same.

## In the region

For the second day in a row, there was not a single artificial intelligence story originating in Latin America: no publications from the multilateral organizations that work on the region, no announcements from ministries or digital agencies, no decisions from data protection authorities, no news from the academic centers that follow the subject. The open version of Latam-GPT is still announced for September with no date. Where there was movement, the region shows up in two opposite ways: in Geneva, with doctrine and leadership; in the dialogue the United States and China are preparing, it does not show up at all. And the subject of that conversation is precisely what the three regional bills in progress (Brazil's PL 2338, Chile's Boletín 16.821-19 and the Colombian bill) leave unresolved: they write articles on reporting serious incidents without saying who has the authority to go in and investigate. If the mechanism the two powers agree on ends up being lab self-regulation plus threat intelligence sharing between two governments, the region is not left out of a negotiation: it is left out of the channel through which information about incidents that affect it will flow.

There is a third piece, and it comes from an unexpected place. Anthropic's settlement with U.S. authors is being paid out against ownership records, and it is failing because of poor records in the country that keeps them best. Latin America does not have that registry: no book chamber in the region keeps a consolidated record of reversion of publishing rights, and collective management societies cover music much better than text. If tomorrow there were an equivalent settlement over a Spanish or Portuguese corpus, there would be no way to determine whom to pay. Along the same lines, Google presented its new music model stating that it was trained exclusively on licensed content: the market is moving toward licensing as a requirement, while the Chilean bill still proposes a data mining exception with opt-out, that is, with the burden of monitoring placed on the author. One more contrast, this time on infrastructure: [HyperVault, a subsidiary of India's TCS, will invest up to $7.41 billion in a one-gigawatt campus in Telangana](https://www.tcs.com/who-we-are/newsroom/press-release/tcs-hypervault-establish-large-scale-ai-data-center-campus-telangana) with domestic capital and operator and the state providing the land, the reverse of the scenario the region is negotiating, where the campus is supplied by the foreign provider.

## Launches

- **[Muse Voice Transcribe](https://research.meta.ai/blog/introducing-muse-voice-transcribe/)** — Meta Superintelligence Labs' first real-time audio model: 3.1% word error rate in English, first place in Artificial Analysis's independent measurements and 0.16 seconds of latency. What is relevant for the region are two capabilities that made earlier systems unworkable: it separates more than twenty simultaneous speakers and handles language switching within a single sentence. It is trained on more than 70 languages, with Spanish among the 25 validated in depth; Portuguese is not on that list. That is exactly the scenario of a court hearing, a city council or an Indigenous consultation. The downside: it does not publish its weights, it is accessible only through an API, and the recording travels to the provider, which is hard to justify for data from a court or a public health system.
- **[Lyria 3.5](https://the-decoder.com/google-brings-ai-music-generation-directly-into-the-gemini-app-with-its-new-lyria-3-5-model/)** — Google brought its music model to the Gemini app and its API, available to all users worldwide: complete high-fidelity stereo songs, with verses, choruses and bridges, up to three minutes long, with genre-guided creation and a vocal or instrumental option. It is interesting as much as a creative tool as a field test of the legal argument: the company says it trained solely on licensed content, without publishing the sources. It remains to be seen how well it understands regional genres and whether that repertoire includes music in Spanish or Portuguese.
