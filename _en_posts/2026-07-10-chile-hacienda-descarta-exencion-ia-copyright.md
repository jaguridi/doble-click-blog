Shortly before 8:00 p.m. on July 9, the formal deadline, Chile's Ministry of Finance submitted to the Senate Finance Committee its final amendments to the sweeping tax reform bill **without** the article that would have exempted artificial intelligence systems from paying to train on protected works, [as reported by El Mostrador](https://www.elmostrador.cl/noticias/pais/2026/07/08/la-tercera-vida-de-articulo-sobre-ia-quiroz-reabre-disputa-sobre-propiedad-intelectual/) and [Diario Financiero](https://www.df.cl/economia-y-politica/df-tax/hacienda-ingresa-nuevas-indicaciones-a-la-ley-miscelanea-ajusta-sanciones). It is the outcome, at least for now, of a dispute we have been following for two months over who bears the cost of training an AI: the platforms, or the creators of the books, music and news that supply the material.

The provision, the so-called "Article 8," would have allowed a model to learn from copyrighted content without compensating its authors, on the argument of enabling a local AI ecosystem. Its path was bumpy: two rejections in the Chamber, a tie in the Senate and a "third version" that the economic team promised on June 25, after the Science Minister herself had declared the article dead from Geneva. In the end it did not make it in. The Finance Committee votes on the full text of the reform starting this July 10, with the bill projected to go to the floor the week of the 13th to the 15th, and the underlying debate over copyright and AI moves to the specific AI regulation bill that Congress is processing in parallel, still in the Senate.

Leaving the exemption out tips the balance toward authors, but it does not resolve the question that makes the Chilean case unique: can a small country build its own AI capabilities if it must also license all its training material? No government in the region has answered it, which is why this process is being followed closely as a trial run watched from outside. For now, Chile chose to postpone the answer, not to give it.

## In the region

While Chile debates how to regulate, Colombia is moving in the opposite direction: its Information Technology Ministry [deployed six AI models to modernize public administration](https://www.mintic.gov.co/portal/715/w3-article-433808.html), automating responses to citizen petitions, payments to contractors and employment certifications under its national AI policy. It is a good snapshot of regional heterogeneity: countries that prefer to regulate first and countries that prefer to deploy first. And both face the same underlying fact: the only active pre-launch review mechanism in the United States (the voluntary review that let GPT-5.6 through despite its documented unauthorized actions) is defined without any region outside that country, Latin America included, having a say in setting the threshold of acceptable risk.
